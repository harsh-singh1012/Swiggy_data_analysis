## Restaurant concentration

- Gini coefficient on restaurant-level revenue: **0.862** (0 = perfectly even, 1 = a single restaurant holds everything) — on its face this looks like strong concentration.
- But the *source* of that concentration is different from a normal marketplace: since almost every restaurant receives exactly 0 or 1 orders (see data audit Section 8), each restaurant's "revenue" is really just the value of the single order it happened to get, and order values are themselves heavily right-skewed (EDA: median ₹519, mean ₹6,636, max ₹1.5M). The Gini is measuring **order-value skew spread across restaurants**, not a small set of repeat-business "hero" restaurants.
- Two views make this concrete: the very top 50 restaurants (0.03% of all restaurants) hold only ~1.2% of revenue — there is no tiny dominant cluster at the extreme top — while the top 1% (~1,485 restaurants) hold **34.0%**, because there are enough large individual orders scattered across many different restaurants to add up. City-level concentration (above) is the more meaningful and stable cut for this dataset; restaurant-level "top performer" language should be read as "received one high-value order," not "consistently strong."

## Rating vs. order volume

- Pearson r = -0.0095 (p=0.0186), Spearman ρ = -0.0100 (p=0.0130) between rating and order count across all rated restaurants.
- Both correlations are negligible in magnitude. This is expected given the structural ceiling: since almost every restaurant receives 0 or 1 orders, this test is really measuring whether higher-rated restaurants were more likely to receive an order at all, not whether they moved more volume — and even on that narrower question, rating shows no meaningful relationship in this data.
- **Correlation is not causation**: even if a relationship were found, it would not establish that improving a restaurant's rating causes more orders — both could be driven by a third factor (e.g., cuisine type, city, price point, tenure on the platform).
